\begin{abstract}
Automated skin lesion classification is a promising application of deep
learning in computer-aided dermatology, with the potential to support
early detection of melanoma and other skin cancers. In this work, we
compare three deep learning approaches for seven-class skin lesion
classification on the publicly available DermaMNIST benchmark
\cite{yang2023medmnist}. Our baseline is a compact three-block
convolutional neural network (CNN) trained from scratch. Method~A
applies full fine-tuning of a pretrained ResNet-18 \cite{he2016deep}
with a standard cross-entropy loss. Method~B extends Method~A with
two design innovations: (i)~RandAugment \cite{cubuk2020randaugment}
for training-time augmentation, and (ii)~Focal Loss
\cite{lin2017focal} with inverse-frequency class weights to address
the inherent class imbalance in DermaMNIST. All models are trained
over three random seeds and evaluated using Balanced Accuracy and
Macro-F1. Our results show that transfer learning (Method~A) yields
a Macro-F1 of $0.5048 \pm 0.0304$, substantially outperforming the
baseline ($0.3510 \pm 0.0014$). Method~B achieves the highest
Balanced Accuracy of $0.6221 \pm 0.0178$, confirming that Focal Loss
with class-weighting better handles minority classes at the cost of
slightly reduced Macro-F1. These findings highlight the importance
of both pretrained features and class-aware loss design for imbalanced
medical image classification. All code is publicly available and
fully reproducible.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Skin cancer is among the most prevalent cancers worldwide, with
melanoma responsible for the majority of skin cancer deaths
\cite{siegel2023cancer}. Early and accurate diagnosis is critical
for patient outcomes, yet visual inspection by dermatologists is
time-consuming and subject to inter-observer variability. Deep
learning-based classification systems offer a scalable complement
to clinical diagnosis.

\textbf{Problem statement.}
Given a dermoscopic image of a skin lesion (input: RGB image), the
model must predict one of seven diagnostic categories (output:
class label $y \in \{0,\ldots,6\}$): actinic keratoses,
basal cell carcinoma, benign keratosis, dermatofibroma, melanoma,
melanocytic nevi, and vascular lesions.

\textbf{Hypothesis.}
We hypothesize that:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Transfer learning (Method~A) will substantially outperform
        the scratch-trained baseline because pretrained ImageNet
        features provide rich descriptors that transfer to dermoscopy.
  \item Method~B will outperform Method~A on Balanced Accuracy because
        Focal Loss with class weights provides a stronger training
        signal for rare minority classes (e.g., dermatofibroma,
        vascular lesion), and RandAugment reduces overfitting on the
        dominant melanocytic nevi class.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Contributions.}
(1)~A modular, reproducible PyTorch codebase for DermaMNIST
classification with full seed control.
(2)~An empirical comparison of three methods under identical
conditions across three random seeds.
(3)~Systematic error analysis via confusion matrix and failure cases.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Deep learning for dermatology.}
Esteva et al.~\cite{esteva2017dermatologist} demonstrated that a CNN
trained on 129,450 clinical images can classify skin cancer at
a level comparable to board-certified dermatologists, motivating the
use of deep learning for skin lesion analysis.

\textbf{MedMNIST benchmark.}
Yang et al.~\cite{yang2023medmnist} introduced MedMNIST v2, a
collection of 18 standardized biomedical image classification
datasets. DermaMNIST, derived from the HAM10000 dataset
\cite{tschandl2018ham10000}, provides an official three-way split
and serves as a reproducible benchmark for our study.

\textbf{Transfer learning in medical imaging.}
Tajbakhsh et al.~\cite{tajbakhsh2016convolutional} showed that
fine-tuning pretrained networks consistently outperforms training
from scratch across medical imaging tasks, even when source and
target domains differ substantially.

\textbf{Focal Loss for class imbalance.}
Lin et al.~\cite{lin2017focal} proposed Focal Loss to address extreme
class imbalance by re-weighting the loss based on prediction
confidence, focusing learning on hard, misclassified examples.
This is directly applicable to DermaMNIST, where melanocytic nevi
constitutes $\approx 67\%$ of training samples.

\textbf{RandAugment.}
Cubuk et al.~\cite{cubuk2020randaugment} proposed RandAugment, which
randomly samples from a set of transformations during training,
reducing overfitting on small medical imaging datasets.

Data integrity was verified by computing MD5 hashes of all images

two splits was empty, confirming no cross-split duplicates exist.

All images are resized to $224\times224$ and normalized using

$\sigma=[0.229, 0.224, 0.225]$). Method~B additionally applies
RandAugment ($N=2$, $M=9$) and random horizontal/vertical flips
during training only.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Baseline CNN}

The baseline is a three-block CNN trained from random initialization.
Each block follows the pattern: Conv~$\rightarrow$~BatchNorm~$\rightarrow$~ReLU~$\rightarrow$~MaxPool(2).
Global Average Pooling (GAP) replaces a large flatten layer to
reduce parameters, followed by a two-layer classifier with
Dropout(0.5). Table~\ref{tab:baseline_shapes} shows the output
shape at each layer.

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\caption{Baseline CNN layer-wise output shapes (batch size = 1)}
\label{tab:baseline_shapes}
\begin{tabular}{llr}
\toprule
\textbf{Layer} & \textbf{Output Shape} & \textbf{Params} \\
\midrule
Input                         & (1, 3, 224, 224)  & —      \\
Block 1 (Conv32+BN+Pool)      & (1, 32, 112, 112) & 928    \\
Block 2 (Conv64+BN+Pool)      & (1, 64, 56, 56)   & 18,560 \\
Block 3 (Conv128+BN+Pool)     & (1, 128, 28, 28)  & 73,984 \\
Global Avg Pool               & (1, 128, 1, 1)    & —      \\
Flatten                       & (1, 128)           & —      \\
FC1 (128$\to$256)+ReLU+Drop   & (1, 256)           & 33,024 \\
FC2 (256$\to$7)               & (1, 7)             & 1,799  \\
\midrule
\textbf{Total}                & —          & \textbf{128,295} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\subsection{Method A: ResNet-18 + Cross-Entropy}

Method~A initializes from ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-18
\cite{he2016deep} (torchvision \texttt{IMAGENET1K\_V1}) with the
final FC layer replaced by a 7-class linear head. All backbone
layers are trained (full fine-tuning). The loss is standard
cross-entropy:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_\text{CE} = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \log p(y_i \mid \mathbf{x}_i)
\end{equation}

\subsection{Method B: ResNet-18 + RandAugment + Focal Loss}

Method~B uses the identical ResNet-18 architecture as Method~A with
two design innovations justified by the class imbalance problem:

\textbf{(1) RandAugment} \cite{cubuk2020randaugment}: $N=2$
randomly chosen augmentation operations (magnitude $M=9$) plus
random horizontal and vertical flips. Orientation flips are
clinically safe for skin lesion imagery.

\textbf{(2) Focal Loss} \cite{lin2017focal} with inverse-frequency
class weights:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_\text{FL}(p_y) = -\alpha_y\,(1 - p_y)^\gamma \log p_y,
\quad \gamma = 2
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_c = N / (C \cdot n_c)$ is the inverse-frequency weight
for class $c$, $N$ is total training samples, $C=7$, and $n_c$ is
the per-class count.

\textbf{Justification.} The 30:1 class imbalance biases vanilla
cross-entropy toward melanocytic nevi predictions. Focal Loss
down-weights easy, correctly-classified majority-class examples and
amplifies gradients from hard, misclassified minority-class examples,
leading to higher Balanced Accuracy at the potential cost of
Macro-F1 (since precision on minority classes may decrease as the
model becomes more recall-oriented).

\section{Experiments and Results}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
All experiments were conducted on a free-tier Google Colab notebook
with an NVIDIA T4 GPU. Model selection (best checkpoint) was
performed using validation loss only; the test set was reserved for
final evaluation. \textit{Balanced Accuracy} and \textit{Macro-F1}
were chosen because both weight all classes equally, appropriate for
the highly imbalanced DermaMNIST distribution.

\subsection{Main Results}

Table~\ref{tab:results} presents the main results over 3 seeds.
Transfer learning (Method~A) achieves a Macro-F1 of
$0.5048 \pm 0.0304$, a $43.8\%$ relative improvement over the
Baseline ($0.3510 \pm 0.0014$), confirming Hypothesis~1. Method~B
achieves the highest Balanced Accuracy of $0.6221 \pm 0.0178$,
a $23.3\%$ improvement over Method~A ($0.5056 \pm 0.0471$), but its
Macro-F1 ($0.4755 \pm 0.0148$) is slightly lower than Method~A.

\section{Discussion and Error Analysis}

\subsection{Hypothesis Evaluation}
Hypothesis~1 is confirmed: Method~A (Macro-F1 = 0.5048) substantially
outperforms the Baseline (0.3510). Hypothesis~2 is partially confirmed:
Method~B achieves the best Balanced Accuracy (0.6221 vs.\ 0.5056),
demonstrating superior minority-class recall, but its Macro-F1 (0.4755)
does not surpass Method~A (0.5048). This trade-off is expected when
Focal Loss aggressively boosts recall for rare classes at the cost of
precision, slightly reducing overall F1.

\subsection{Confusion Matrix Analysis}

Figure~\ref{fig:cm} shows the row-normalised confusion matrix for
Method~B (seed~42). Key observations:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textit{Melanocytic nevi} achieves the highest per-class
        recall due to its dominant representation ($\sim$67\% of data).
  \item \textit{Dermatofibroma} (F1 = 0.27) is most frequently confused
        with \textit{benign keratosis} and \textit{melanoma}, likely
        due to overlapping pigmentation patterns at 224$\times$224.
  \item \textit{Melanoma} is confused with \textit{melanocytic nevi}
        in a clinically significant fraction of cases, as both share
        irregular pigment networks.
  \item \textit{Vascular lesions} achieves a recall of 0.86, suggesting
        that Focal Loss effectively focuses training on this rare class.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Failure Case Analysis}

Figure~\ref{fig:fail} shows three representative failure cases of
Method~B on the test set.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{failure_cases_method_b_seed42.png}
\caption{Three failure cases of Method~B (seed 42). Each panel shows
the image, true label, predicted label, confidence scores, and a
failure hypothesis.}
\label{fig:fail}
\end{figure}

The failures cluster around three recurring patterns:
(1)~\textit{Melanoma} misclassified as \textit{melanocytic nevi}
— both share similar dermoscopic patterns (irregular pigment network,
dark colouration); (2)~\textit{Vascular lesion} confused with
\textit{dermatofibroma} — both appear as small, flat reddish spots
at native 28$\times$28 resolution; and (3)~\textit{Actinic keratosis}
confused with \textit{benign keratosis} — both present as flat,
scaly lesions with similar colour profiles.

\subsection{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
  \item DermaMNIST native resolution is $28\times28$; upsampling may
        introduce aliasing artifacts that degrade classification.
  \item No ablation study separates the individual contributions of
        RandAugment and Focal Loss within Method~B.
  \item Generalization beyond DermaMNIST (e.g., full ISIC 2019 dataset)
        is not evaluated.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

We presented a comparative study of three deep learning approaches for
skin lesion classification on DermaMNIST. Transfer learning from
pretrained ResNet-18 (Method~A) yields Macro-F1 = $0.5048 \pm 0.0304$,
a 43.8\% relative improvement over the scratch-trained baseline,
confirming the value of pretrained image representations. Method~B
achieves the best Balanced Accuracy ($0.6221 \pm 0.0178$), confirming
that Focal Loss with inverse-frequency class weights improves
minority-class recall on imbalanced dermatological datasets, at a modest
cost to Macro-F1. Error analysis reveals that melanoma/nevi confusion
and low-sample-count classes remain the primary challenge.

Future work includes: (i)~ablation studies isolating the contribution
of RandAugment versus Focal Loss; (ii)~experiments with higher-resolution
inputs; (iii)~contrastive learning or mixup strategies for minority
classes; and (iv)~incorporation of patient metadata as auxiliary inputs.
